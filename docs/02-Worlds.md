# Worlds

I think this is a particularly promising direction for TerrificTravel. It gives the game something more interesting than ordinary time travel: travel between different conceptions of reality.

The really interesting possibility is that the player doesn't merely visit the Earth as it existed at different times. The player visits different worlds, each of which represents an attempt by humans to understand, describe, and explain reality.

A location can therefore have several simultaneous identities:

- In the modern scientific world, it is an island in the Mediterranean.
- In an ancient Greek world, it might be at the edge of the known world, surrounded by mythological geography.
- In a medieval Christian world, it might be situated within a religiously meaningful cosmological order.
- In a Polynesian navigational world, its position might be understood through stars, ocean currents, winds, and ancestral routes.
- In a mythological world, it might be inhabited by gods, spirits, monsters, or supernatural beings.

And these aren't necessarily just different descriptions of the same scenery. They can have different geography, inhabitants, causal relationships, and physical rules.

I would provisionally call these World Models.

One important design suggestion: I wouldn't make the world-model axis strictly chronological. Some models coexist, some evolve independently, some influence each other, and some are reconstructions of much older traditions. A partially ordered dimension is a much better representation than a simple timeline.

Below is a deliberately large catalogue. I'm including scientific theories, historical geographical knowledge, cosmologies, mythologies, and systems of navigation. They are different kinds of things, but that is precisely what could make the game interesting.

Mythological world

The island might be a sacred place, a dwelling of a deity, or an entrance to another realm.

The player can move between these representations while remaining at the same underlying location.

This creates interesting puzzles:

- A cave exists in all worlds, but has different inhabitants.
- A mountain is ordinary in one world and sacred in another.
- A river is geographically real in one model but becomes a boundary between worlds in another.
- A creature encountered in one world may be invisible or nonexistent in another.

## B. Different physical laws

I think this is potentially one of the most exciting features.

Different world models need not merely change their appearance. They can change the rules governing what is possible.

| World model         | Possible game rules                                         |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Modern scientific   | Ordinary physics                                            |
| Aristotelian        | Natural motion, distinct terrestrial and celestial physics  |
| Ptolemaic           | Celestial spheres and geocentric planetary motion           |
| Newtonian           | Absolute space and time, classical mechanics                |
| Relativistic        | Time dilation and curved spacetime                          |
| Quantum-inspired    | Probability, superposition, observation                     |
| Puranic             | Multiple cosmic levels and enormous cycles of time          |
| Norse               | Connected realms, cosmic structures, supernatural beings    |
| Greek mythological  | Divine intervention, transformations, prophecies            |
| Egyptian            | Cyclic solar journeys, underworld passages                  |
| Aztec               | Cosmic cycles and successive worlds                         |
| Dreaming traditions | Ancestral connections between landscape, beings, and events |
| Flatland            | Only two spatial dimensions are accessible                  |

There is an important distinction here: some of these are historical physical theories, while others are mythological or religious cosmologies. Their rules should be represented as internally coherent game mechanics, not as competing scientific explanations.

## C. Different models can reveal different information

This could be a very nice exploration mechanism.

A player might discover something in one world that cannot be understood there, but becomes meaningful after switching to another.

For example:

1. In the modern world, the player discovers an unusual mountain formation.
2. In the ancient Greek world, the mountain is associated with a mythological creature.
3. In another world, the mountain is a sacred geographical feature.
4. In the geological past, the player discovers how the mountain was formed.

The player gradually builds a more complete understanding by combining different perspectives.

This makes learning a consequence of exploration rather than a separate educational activity.

# A suggestion about the underlying architecture

I would actually distinguish three things that are somewhat mixed together in the catalogue.

Dimension 1

Geographical and spatial representation

Where are things? What does the map look like? What is connected to what?

Dimension 2

Historical and cultural conception

When, where, and by whom was this model developed? What knowledge and traditions does it represent?

Dimension 3

Ontological and physical rules

What exists? What can happen? What are the laws governing objects, creatures, space, and time?

This distinction might be important because the same geographical map can be interpreted through different cosmologies, and the same cosmology can have several geographical representations.

In other words, you may eventually discover that your four-dimensional world is actually a convenient visualization of a much richer structure.

The proposed 4D space might be:

\\[ W=(x,y,t,m) \\]

where:

- \\(x,y\\): geographical position on the map;
- \\(t\\): historical or temporal position;
- \\(m\\): world-model identity.

The temporal coordinate need not be a simple linear timeline. Some models will be historically related; others will be independent or contemporaneous.

And the physical rules could be functions of the world model:

\\[ \text{Physics}=P(m) \\]

This is just a conceptual starting point, not a commitment to a particular implementation.

One final thought: I would resist the temptation to define one world as the true world and the others as merely incorrect approximations.

For the purposes of TerrificTravel, the player should be able to explore how different models organize reality, what they explain, what they leave unexplained, and what becomes possible inside them.

The modern scientific model can have a special role as our best empirically supported account of physical reality without depriving other models of their imaginative, historical, cultural, and narrative significance.

That, I think, is where the educational and entertainment possibilities become particularly interesting.
