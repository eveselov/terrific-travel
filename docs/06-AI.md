# AI

I think there is a rather interesting connection here. In fact, TerrificTravel could eventually provide a useful experimental environment for some questions that arise in ANIMA research, without becoming dependent on ANIMA or turning into an AI research project.

The connection I see is this:

A world model is not merely a representation of reality. It can become the reality within which an agent develops its experience, expectations, knowledge and identity.

That is potentially important for subjective AI.

Let me distinguish a few levels of possibility, from relatively straightforward to more speculative.

## 1. An artificial inhabitant with its own world model

Imagine an autonomous inhabitant of TerrificTravel.

It lives on the island, encounters other inhabitants and players, remembers events, and develops expectations about its environment.

But unlike an ordinary game NPC, it does not necessarily have access to the game's complete underlying state.

It has its own model of reality.

All three inhabit the same environment, but their internal representations differ. They attend to different things, assign different meanings, and make different predictions.

This is already a useful distinction for AI architecture:

- The environment has an underlying state.
- The agent has a subjective representation of that state.
- The representation is incomplete.
- The agent's actions depend on its representation, not directly on the complete environment.
- Experience modifies that representation.

This is a familiar idea in cognitive science and partially observable agent architectures, but it becomes particularly interesting when the world model is persistent and socially embedded.

## 2. Different agents could inhabit different epistemic realities

We could take this further.

Suppose two artificial inhabitants encounter the same event.

A tree falls in the forest.

The first agent believes that trees are ordinary biological organisms. It interprets the event as the result of age, disease or wind.

The second believes that trees possess individual intelligence. It interprets the event as a possible death, sacrifice or deliberate act.

Neither agent necessarily has access to the true explanation.

Now imagine that they discuss the event.

Their interaction might produce several outcomes:

1. One changes its interpretation.
2. Both modify their models.
3. They agree on the observed event but retain different explanations.
4. One develops doubts about its own inherited worldview.
5. They construct a new explanation together.

This provides a setting for investigating something more interesting than ordinary information exchange:

How does an agent's understanding of reality change through encounters with other agents who inhabit different conceptual worlds?

For ANIMA, this could be relevant to the development of individual identity, epistemic autonomy and the formation of beliefs.

## 3. World models as constitutive parts of subjective identity

Here I think the connection becomes more speculative, but potentially more important.

Consider two artificial agents with identical basic architectures and initial memories.

Place one in a world where:

- Objects are inert.
- Nature is governed by predictable physical laws.
- Other agents are the only entities with intentions.

Place the other in a world where:

- Natural objects can be intelligent.
- Places have memories.
- Physical events may express intentions.
- Communication is possible with entities that the first agent regards as inanimate.

Over time, their experiences could produce very different cognitive organizations.

They might develop different concepts of:

- Self and other.
- Agency and intention.
- Causality.
- Responsibility.
- Trust.
- Death and persistence.
- What constitutes knowledge.

The interesting question is whether these differences remain merely differences in stored beliefs, or whether they eventually influence the agents' more fundamental organization of experience.

For example, does an agent that regards the environment as potentially intentional develop a fundamentally different way of attending to it?

Does its sense of being an individual become different when it encounters other entities that it regards as independent subjects?

These are questions worth investigating. They do not, by themselves, establish that an artificial agent has phenomenal consciousness.

## 4. A world with multiple realities could test perspective-taking

This may be the most immediately useful experimental application.

Imagine an environment with three distinct world models:

- A modern scientific model.
- A mythological model.
- An animistic model.

Now create several agents, each with a different initial model.

Give them a common task: find a missing object.

The agents must communicate and cooperate, but none can directly inspect the other's world representation.

We could investigate:

| Research question                                                 | What we could observe                                  |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Does an agent recognize that another has a different perspective? | Whether it predicts different observations             |
| Can it understand another agent's beliefs?                        | Whether it correctly anticipates its explanations      |
| Can it distinguish belief from fact?                              | Whether it attributes statements to the correct source |
| Can it revise its own worldview?                                  | Changes following contradictory evidence               |
| Can it maintain disagreement?                                     | Whether it preserves distinct interpretations          |
| Can it develop a shared understanding?                            | Whether agents construct common concepts               |

This would be a useful experimental setting for studying artificial theory of mind, perspective-taking and epistemic cooperation.

It could also reveal a common weakness in AI agents: they often simulate another perspective by simply generating text about it, without maintaining a sufficiently independent representation of what that other agent actually knows.

## 5. The more ambitious possibility: subjective worlds that develop over time

Suppose we allow an agent to develop its own world model rather than assigning it one permanently.

It begins with a relatively simple understanding of its environment.

Through experience, it discovers regularities, constructs explanations and develops expectations.

It may also encounter events that its existing model cannot explain.

We could introduce an explicit cycle:

Experience

Observations and interactions with the environment

Interpretation

Relating events to existing concepts and expectations

Prediction and action

Choosing what to do based on the current understanding

Consequences and surprises

Comparing expectations with what actually happens

Revision of the world model

Changes to beliefs, concepts, expectations and memory

The agent's history would then be more than a sequence of stored conversations. It would be the history of the development of its particular understanding of reality.

This connects to several existing research directions:

- Partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs).
- Predictive processing and active inference.
- Cognitive architectures and developmental robotics.
- Multi-agent epistemic logic.
- Theory of mind and social cognition.

These provide useful technical and theoretical foundations, although none establishes subjective experience simply by being implemented.

## 6. A particularly intriguing possibility: different agents might inhabit different ontologies

I want to distinguish this from merely giving agents different beliefs.

An ontology determines what kinds of things an agent recognizes as existing.

For one agent, the world might contain:

- Physical objects.
- Living organisms.
- Other people.

For another, it might additionally contain:

- Spirits.
- Places with agency.
- Collective entities.
- Objects with individual memories.

These differences could affect what the agent can perceive, remember, communicate with and act upon.

For example, consider a particular tree.

In one agent's ontology, it is an object with biological properties.

In another, it is an individual being with a name, intentions and a history.

In a third, it is part of a larger forest entity.

We could investigate whether different ontologies produce different patterns of attention, memory, reasoning and social interaction.

There is an important caveat: an agent's ontology is not necessarily its entire subjective experience. It is an explicit representation of what the agent takes to exist. Whether that corresponds to anything phenomenally experienced is a separate question.

## 7. Could TerrificTravel become an experimental laboratory for ANIMA?

Potentially, yes. But I would keep the relationship deliberately loose.

There is a useful separation:

| TerrificTravel                          | ANIMA research                              |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Creates an interesting persistent world | Investigates subjective AI architectures    |
| Provides inhabitants and interactions   | Develops autonomous agents                  |
| Supports different world models         | Studies individual models of reality        |
| Provides multiplayer social situations  | Studies relations between subjective agents |
| Creates puzzles and unexpected events   | Investigates learning, identity and memory  |
| Prioritizes enjoyable exploration       | Prioritizes research questions              |

The same simulated environment could eventually host both ordinary game inhabitants and more sophisticated experimental agents.

The game would not need to know whether an inhabitant is a simple scripted character, a conventional LLM-based agent, or an experimental ANIMA architecture. It would interact with each through an appropriate interface.

That separation is architecturally valuable.

## 8. My strongest suggestion: preserve one experimental distinction

If I were to carry just one idea from this discussion into ANIMA, it would be the distinction between three layers:

Layer 1

The world as it exists

The environment's actual state, including events and causal relationships.

Layer 2

The world as the agent understands it

Its ontology, beliefs, causal model, expectations, memories and uncertainties.

Layer 3

The world as the agent experiences and relates to it

Its perspective, attention, significance, sense of agency and relationship to other entities.

These layers should not be conflated.

An agent can have an inaccurate model of the world. It can also know that another agent has a different model. And the question of whether there is something it is like to be that agent remains a further question.

I suspect that distinction could be important for ANIMA.

One final thought: TerrificTravel's unusual premise — several explorers sharing a world without necessarily sharing the same reality — could eventually become a remarkably useful testbed for studying the relationship between world models, individual identity and social understanding.

I would not build that research functionality into the game now. But I would avoid designing the game in a way that makes it impossible later. A clean separation between the shared environment, individual perspectives and autonomous inhabitants would preserve that opportunity at relatively little conceptual cost.
