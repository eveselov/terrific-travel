# World Models

I think this is a particularly interesting direction, especially given that the two players enjoy scientific thinking. It could give TerrificTravel a distinctive intellectual dimension: exploring not only different maps of the world, but different explanations of why the world behaves as it does.

And I agree that we shouldn't make it a requirement for every world model. It could be an optional layer of depth, introduced gradually.

## 1. A world model includes more than geography

We could think of each world model as having three related aspects:

1. World structure: What exists, and how is it arranged? Is the Earth a sphere, a flat disc, or the centre of a system of celestial spheres?
2. Laws of nature: How do things behave? What makes objects fall, stars move, winds blow, or living things grow?
3. Nature of things: What kinds of entities exist? Are objects merely material things, or can they possess intelligence, intention, memory, or agency?

These aspects need not always be consistent with one another. Indeed, some interesting historical worldviews combine ideas that modern science would regard as incompatible.

An invented alternative physics

Gravity might point towards the nearest mountain, light might travel along curved paths, or sound might carry messages across enormous distances. This need not represent a historical belief; it could be an imaginative model designed specifically for the game.

One important distinction: the historical models should be presented in their proper cultural and historical context. For example, the crystalline heavens have a history in ancient and medieval cosmology, but the particular image of stars as silver nails is better treated as a possible imaginative interpretation unless we attach it to a specific source.

## 3. The really interesting part: two players can test different physics

Imagine the two players standing on the same mountain, but viewing it through different world models.

A small experiment: the falling stone

Explorer A — Ancient natural philosophy

Objects seek their natural place. A stone falls because its natural place is below, towards the centre of the world.

[Experimento de la Torre Inclinada de Pisa de Galileo: 'Ciencia', 'Historia' | StudySmarter](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/maYZN3zLuJH7gPSceuT6o-tFRnOCdJTSMys31vjDjig--0nCz-ihAlK6_9O1m3Iq5HOpwReBpXgw5QT1VX160g2C0SkZhEIGdGD50fXv-y3xGS5KKxLDSfU9PFmNTHfJzvZNKrVmCLjsQoU7IlKu-96lXQw80PnDT9TbKSqKU9E?purpose=inline)

Explorer B — Modern physics

The stone accelerates because of gravitational interaction with the Earth. Its trajectory can be predicted mathematically.

They release two stones simultaneously. Both see the same event, but explain it differently. They can compare predictions and observations.

This could be extended to more surprising experiments:

- The stars: Are they distant suns, lights fixed to a celestial sphere, or beings communicating with the Earth?
- The wind: Is it caused by atmospheric pressure differences, or is it the breath of a divine or intelligent entity?
- The forest: Is a tree a biological organism, or does it also possess an individual personality?
- The sea: Is it a body of water governed by physical forces, or a domain inhabited and governed by powerful beings?

The important design opportunity is that different models need not merely change descriptions. They can change what actions are possible and what answers players can discover.

## 4. A particularly promising multiplayer mechanic

I would consider introducing a concept called the experiment.

An experiment is something the players can do together, even if they inhabit different world models.

For example:

1. One player observes a star through a mythological model and learns its traditional name and significance.
2. The other observes the same star through a modern astronomical model and measures its position.
3. They exchange observations.
4. They discover that their descriptions refer to the same celestial object, but their interpretations differ.

Or a more challenging example:

- One player encounters an apparently intelligent tree.
- The other sees an ordinary tree.
- They perform experiments together to discover whether the tree is actually communicating, and whether communication is possible across world models.

This creates a nice distinction between observation, interpretation and explanation. It is also a genuinely scientific way of playing.

## 5. I would keep the underlying architecture deliberately modest

We don't need to simulate an entirely different universe for each model. Initially, a world model could simply define a handful of properties:

| Property             | Example                               |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Cosmology            | Geocentric                            |
| Gravity              | Objects seek their natural place      |
| Sky                  | Crystalline celestial spheres         |
| Stars                | Fixed luminous objects                |
| Nature               | Some natural entities are intelligent |
| Special interactions | Conversation with a river spirit      |

The game engine can then use these properties to determine descriptions, available interactions and puzzle solutions.

Some properties could be purely narrative. Others could have actual mechanical consequences. We should not attempt to implement a general-purpose alternative physics engine.

## 6. One idea I particularly like

There might be a distinction between the rules of a world and the beliefs of its inhabitants.

Suppose a player enters a medieval cosmological model. The inhabitants believe the heavens are crystalline. But perhaps, through exploration, the player discovers evidence that challenges their belief.

Does the world actually behave according to the inhabitants' understanding, or are they simply mistaken?

We could allow several possibilities:

- The model's physical rules really are different.
- The inhabitants have an incomplete understanding of their own world.
- The player's interpretation is incomplete too.
- Different explanations can coexist without the game immediately declaring one of them correct.

This would make the world intellectually interesting without turning it into a lesson in the history of science.

My favourite design principle emerging from this discussion is:

Players should be able to investigate not only what is happening in a world, but what kind of world they are inhabiting.

That could become one of TerrificTravel's defining features. And it gives two scientifically curious young explorers a reason to discuss, experiment, disagree, and occasionally surprise each other.
